5 Key Findings

The most critical insights from these IUCN reports.

01

Tourism is the Most Waste-Intensive Sector Per Capita

Tourists in the Caribbean generate 6.22 kg of waste per day — 3.5× more than residents (1.77 kg). In the Pacific, tourists produce 1.51 kg/day vs 0.14 kg/day for households — a staggering 10× ratio. Per visitor plastic disposal is 0.45 kg/day in the Caribbean and 0.17 kg/day in the Pacific. With hundreds of thousands of annual visitors, tourism waste overwhelms the limited waste infrastructure of these small islands.

02

Household & Commercial Sectors Drive the Largest Total Leakage Volumes

Despite tourism's high per-capita intensity, the sheer number of residents means households contribute the most total leakage: 1,984 tonnes/year in the Caribbean (64% of all leakage) and 4,294 tonnes/year in the Pacific (45%). Combined with commercial waste, these two sectors account for over 83% of all plastic leakage in both regions. Inadequate waste collection — only 56-60% coverage in parts of Grenada, Samoa and Vanuatu — is a key driver.

03

"Other" Plastics and PET Are the Most Leaked Polymers

"Other" plastics (multi-layered packaging, polycarbonate) are the #1 leaked polymer in both regions — 908 tonnes in the Caribbean and 3,319 tonnes in the Pacific — because they cannot be recycled. PET is the #2 polymer leaked overall, driven by beverage bottles (water and soft drinks) that represent 18-23% of all plastic waste. PS/Styrofoam is a disproportionately large problem in the Pacific (1,481 tonnes leaked) compared to the Caribbean (281 tonnes).

04

Caribbean Islands Dispose of Far More Waste Per Person Than Pacific Islands

Caribbean SIDS dispose of 1.77 kg/person/day of total waste compared to just 0.14 kg/person/day in the Pacific — nearly 13 times more. This correlates with higher GDP per capita: Antigua and Barbuda is a high-income economy, while Vanuatu is lower-middle-income. Despite lower per-capita rates, the Pacific has higher total leakage (9,614 vs 3,087 tonnes) due to larger populations and 50-58% of waste being completely unmanaged in Samoa and Vanuatu.

05

Virtually No Recycling Infrastructure Exists — The Tap Is Wide Open

Only 59 tonnes of plastic were recycled across all three Caribbean countries in 2020 — just 0.5% of the 11,779 tonnes disposed. Samoa and Vanuatu have zero active plastic recyclers. These islands import vast quantities of plastic (14,595 tonnes Caribbean; 43,316 tonnes Pacific) with almost no pathways to process it. A Container Deposit Levy at 80% compliance could prevent up to 451 tonnes of PET/HDPE leakage in the Caribbean and 1,033 tonnes of PET leakage in the Pacific.

Recommendations to Fight Plastic Waste

Evidence-based actions that could meaningfully reduce plastic pollution in SIDS.

🏷️

1. Implement Container Deposit Levies (CDLs)

A CDL targeting PET and HDPE bottles at 80% compliance could divert ~451 tonnes/yr in the Caribbean and ~2,200 tonnes/yr in the Pacific from leakage. This is the single highest-impact, most proven policy intervention available. Regional coordination through OECS (Caribbean) and SPREP (Pacific) could create economies of scale for recyclable material exports.

🚢

2. Tax and Manage Tourism Waste at Source

Expand environmental levies on cruise passengers and air arrivals (Saint Lucia's XCD 4.08/visitor model). Mandate waste segregation at all hotels, resorts, ports and airports. Centrally manage cruise ship waste through regional bodies. Adopt the "Palau Pledge" model — tourists sign environmental commitments upon arrival. Ban single-use plastics in tourism facilities.

♻️

3. Build Regional Recycling Hubs

Individual islands cannot achieve economies of scale for recycling. Establish OECS and SPREP-coordinated regional recycling hubs where collected PET, HDPE and other recyclables are aggregated across islands for export. The Moana Taka Partnership (Pacific) already provides free shipping for recyclable waste — extend this model and pair it with collection infrastructure.

🚫

4. Expand Single-Use Plastic Bans to Target "Other" Polymers

Existing bans focus on bags and Styrofoam, but "Other" plastics (multi-layered packaging) are the #1 leaked polymer. Extend bans to multi-layered sachets and non-recyclable flexible packaging. Incentivise alternatives through zero-tariff imports of biodegradable containers (Saint Lucia's approach). Push for extended producer responsibility legislation.

🗑️

5. Achieve Universal Waste Collection Coverage

With 40-50% of waste unmanaged in parts of Grenada, Samoa and Vanuatu, expanding collection services is foundational. Implement dry-wet waste segregation paired with industrial composting (organics are 35-53% of waste). Prepaid bag systems (Vanuatu model) can fund collection. Prioritise peri-urban and rural areas where most unmanaged waste leaks into waterways.

🐟

6. Tackle Fisheries Plastic Through Gear ID & Observer Programs

Mandate gear identification and recovery as a fishing licence requirement. Develop fishing-gear recapture schemes that incentivise fishers to bring back damaged gear. Deploy fisheries observers (Pacific model) to monitor waste dumping. Provide port facilities for net repair and storage. Vanuatu's 82% share of Pacific fisheries leakage highlights the urgent need for port waste infrastructure.

📊

7. Invest in Data Infrastructure & Education

Most islands lack weighbridges, waste tracking and import/export monitoring. Implement 10-digit harmonised customs codes for plastics. Include waste questions in national censuses. Launch intensive education campaigns for residents (littering), fishers (ghost gear impacts) and tourists (pre-arrival environmental briefings). Data enables better policy; education enables behaviour change.

👶

8. Address the Nappy/Diaper Crisis

Hygiene waste (nappies/diapers and sanitary items) accounts for 33.7% of all Pacific plastic waste — a staggering 9,568 tonnes/year. In the Caribbean, nappies are a top household plastic in Grenada and Saint Lucia. Promote reusable cloth nappies through subsidies and awareness programmes. This single intervention could dramatically reduce both disposal volumes and landfill pressure.
